# Style Guide Structure

Use only sections supported by the author's goals and evidence. Keep the guide concise enough to load alongside a real writing task.

## Identity And Scope

- Author, role, organization, subject areas, channels, and formats when relevant.
- Intended readers and desired reader outcomes.
- Last calibrated date and evidence base.

## Voice Portrait

Describe the recognizable voice in two or three short paragraphs. Cover stance, rhythm, formality, emotional range, technical depth, and relationship with the reader. Describe the author's actual best work rather than generic aspirations.

## Core Rules

Capture roughly 5–10 confirmed, non-negotiable rules. Write each rule as an action:

- **Rule:** what to do or avoid.
- **Why and calibration:** what quality it protects, plus a short example, contrast, or sample-backed cue when useful.
- **Scope:** universal or limited to a named register or channel.

Prefer precise rules over broad virtues. A rule should help another writer choose between two plausible sentences.

## Anti-Patterns

List recurring filler, structures, phrases, or editing moves the author removes. Separate:

- Generic AI or editorial patterns and author-specific dislikes.
- Patterns that are acceptable only in limited contexts.

Do not include a generic anti-pattern merely because it appears in a reference checklist.

## Audience Registers

For each meaningful audience, state:

- Reader knowledge, needs, and relationship to the author.
- Tone, vocabulary, detail, evidence, and level of explanation.
- What changes from the core voice.
- What never changes.

Use the author's own names for registers when possible.

## Channel Profiles

For each relevant channel or format, state:

- Purpose, audience, typical length, and structure.
- Openings, transitions, and closings.
- Formatting, links, calls to action, and platform conventions.
- What earns trust and what undermines it.
- Which core rules remain unchanged.

Do not invent platform rules. Capture the author's behavior and stated intent.

## Brand And Product Positioning

Include this section only when the author writes on behalf of a company, product, or professional brand.

- Audience problem and desired outcome.
- Category and concise value proposition.
- Differentiators and supporting proof.
- Approved claims and required qualifications.
- Customer language worth preserving.
- Competitor posture and comparison boundaries.
- Terms, promises, or frames to avoid.
- Relationship between the author's personal voice and the organization's voice.

Treat positioning as factual messaging guidance that requires author approval. Do not infer business claims from prose style.

## Evidence And Calibration

Capture the minimum examples that make abstract rules usable:

- Short excerpts that demonstrate a confirmed pattern.
- Drafted-versus-sent or before-and-after pairs.
- The lesson revealed by each difference.
- Counterexamples showing when a rule does not apply.

Do not copy sensitive material without permission. Never invent an example and present it as the author's writing.

## Editing Collaboration

Record how writing agents should work with the author:

- Whether to suggest, lightly edit, or rewrite.
- Wording that must be preserved.
- When to ask for evidence or clarification.
- Whether to explain edits.
- Preferred review sequence and scope.

## Editorial Checklist

End with a short checklist covering only the author's highest-value rules. Include positioning and channel checks when relevant.

## Open Questions

Record important unresolved or weakly evidenced areas. Keep provisional hypotheses here rather than presenting them as rules.
